Human Race Motor

A city grown by rules instead of drawn by hand: cellular automata generate unit towers, the best are selected as the shell, and a space city takes shape around four zones and walkways in zero gravity.

Technology and nature are two routes through the long river of human civilisation, each moving forward on its own track until they meet at the far end. The history of technology is a process of constantly stealing fire; where people gather, everyday city life becomes its most authentic exhibition.

Here the city is generated rather than drawn. Cellular automata, which can simulate how complex systems evolve in space and time, grow a field of unit towers. Selected by set rules, the towers become the outer shell that houses the city’s functional blocks, organised into four zones and threaded by walkways.

      02How cities grow

      The research sets the space race beside a century of urban models, from the City Beautiful and the Garden City to the sustainable city.

      Four cities show how blocks change over time: in Melbourne plots consolidate from the economic centre out to the fringe; in Barcelona blocks group into communities and then urban areas; Paris opens wide channels so the city can breathe; Manhattan grows vertically, up and into the basement.

      03Unit towers and zones

      Among the many structures generated, the final unit towers were chosen by the selection rules to serve as the outer boundary: the shell of the suspended part of the design, housing the functional blocks and their combinations.

      04Space between buildings

      In a society that only focuses on work and production, housing grows layer by layer around the workplace and takes over every open space. Yet people always find the space that has not been built on yet and make it a place to meet; when it is built over, they find new space.

      Without gravity, people can move between buildings and districts at will, while the city keeps fixed lines of transport. The walkways above the transport pipes are among the few places left for people to walk and talk.
